20 typical foods you must try on the French Riviera

From the markets of Nice to the harbours of Menton, Antibes and Saint-Tropez: discover the dishes, flavours and sweet treats that make the Côte d’Azur so delicious.

An independent food guide by Inside Riviera · Côte d’Azur, France

The French Riviera is famous for sunshine and beautiful seaside restaurants, but its food deserves just as much attention. The cooking here is shaped by the Mediterranean, the hills behind the coast and centuries of trade with neighbouring Italy. Expect olive oil, tomatoes, garlic, anchovies, fresh vegetables, herbs, seafood and citrus. In Nice and the eastern Riviera, you will find a distinctive local cuisine; further west, Provençal dishes and the flavours of the Var become increasingly prominent. Here are 20 specialities to look out for — and the best places along the coast to get to know them.

The flavours of Nice and the eastern Riviera

Nice has one of France’s most distinctive regional food traditions. Many of its best-known dishes began as inexpensive, satisfying food for workers, market shoppers and families.

01
The street-food classic

Socca

Socca is a large, thin pancake made from chickpea flour, water, olive oil and salt, cooked in a very hot oven until the edges are crisp and the centre stays tender. It arrives in irregular pieces, usually with black pepper. It is simple, naturally plant-based and best eaten piping hot. In Nice, look for it around the Cours Saleya market and in the old town, where it is a favourite snack between meals.

Order it: Fresh from the oven, with a generous twist of pepper. Avoid letting it sit around — the contrast between crisp edge and soft middle is part of the pleasure.
02
The famous salad

Salade niçoise

The real salade niçoise is a celebration of Mediterranean ingredients rather than a generic salad with tuna. Traditional versions bring together tomatoes, small black olives from Nice, anchovies, hard-boiled egg, radishes, spring onion or other seasonal raw vegetables, basil and olive oil. Recipes are debated passionately, and cooked vegetables such as potatoes and green beans are often considered departures from the classic local style.

Order it: At a traditional Niçois restaurant, and ask whether the kitchen follows a classic recipe. Tuna is common today, but anchovies are deeply rooted in the original tradition.
03
The perfect picnic

Pan bagnat

Think of pan bagnat as salade niçoise tucked into a round bread roll. The bread is rubbed or moistened with olive oil and filled with tomatoes, anchovies or tuna, hard-boiled egg, olives, raw vegetables and sometimes basil. The name is often translated as “bathed bread”, referring to the way the juices and oil soak into the loaf. It is made for a beach picnic or an easy lunch before exploring the old town.

Order it: Look for one made fresh, with good bread and ripe tomatoes. It should be juicy, not dry.
04
A savoury tart

Pissaladière

This onion tart from the Nice area is made with a bread-like dough, slowly softened onions, olive oil and usually anchovies and small black olives. It is related to the wider family of Mediterranean onion and anchovy breads, but has its own Niçois character. You will find it in bakeries, market stalls and traditional snack bars, often sold by the slice.

Order it: Warm or at room temperature as a light lunch, aperitif snack or picnic bite.
05
Stuffed vegetables

Petits farcis niçois

Small vegetables such as tomatoes, courgettes, peppers and onions are hollowed out, filled with a savoury mixture and baked. The stuffing often includes breadcrumbs, herbs, cheese and sometimes meat, with recipes varying from family to family. It is a lovely example of the Riviera’s practical home cooking: seasonal vegetables turned into a generous main course.

Order it: Look for a seasonal version in a Niçois restaurant or at a traiteur. Ask what the filling contains, as recipes vary.
06
A local sweet-and-savoury debate

Tourte de blettes

Swiss chard is a familiar ingredient in Niçois cooking, and tourte de blettes is one of the region’s most intriguing specialities. The savoury version combines chard with ingredients such as rice, cheese, eggs and herbs. The sweet version may include apples, pine nuts, raisins and sometimes a little rum, with icing sugar on top. Do not be surprised to find both versions in local bakeries.

Order it: Try a small slice of the sweet version with coffee, then seek out the savoury one as a starter or light meal.
07
A seasonal snack

Beignets de fleurs de courgette

Courgette flowers are dipped in a light batter and fried until crisp. They are delicate, fragrant and best eaten straight away, while the batter is still airy. They appear on menus across the Riviera and Provence when the flowers are in season, sometimes stuffed with a soft cheese or herb mixture.

Order it: As a starter to share. They are seasonal, so ask whether the flowers are fresh.

Provençal dishes you will find along the coast

As you travel west from Nice towards Antibes, Cannes and the Var, the food reflects the wider flavours of Provence: vegetables, herbs, olive oil, slow cooking and sunny market produce.

08
The Mediterranean vegetable classic

Ratatouille

Ratatouille is a slow-cooked dish of summer vegetables — typically aubergine, courgette, peppers, tomatoes and onion — with garlic, olive oil and herbs. The vegetables may be cooked together or prepared separately before being combined. It can be served warm or at room temperature, as a side dish or a light main course.

Order it: In summer, when tomatoes and courgettes are at their best. A good version should taste of the vegetables, not just tomato sauce.
09
Slow-cooked comfort food

Daube provençale

Daube is a rich Provençal stew, traditionally made with beef slowly braised in red wine with carrots, onions, garlic, herbs and sometimes orange peel. It is a cooler-weather dish and a reminder that Riviera food is not all salads and seafood. In Nice, you may see a local version served with fresh pasta such as ravioli or gnocchi.

Order it: When you want something hearty. Look for it on traditional menus in autumn and winter.
10
A bowl of Provence

Soupe au pistou

This vegetable and bean soup is finished with pistou, a fragrant mixture of basil, garlic and olive oil, sometimes with cheese. It is related to Italian pesto but traditionally does not rely on pine nuts. Recipes vary, with seasonal vegetables, beans and small pasta often added. It is a classic summer dish, especially when the garden produce is plentiful.

Order it: If you see it on a seasonal menu, especially at a family-run restaurant. The basil should smell fresh and vibrant.
11
Olives and aperitif

Tapenade

Tapenade is a Provençal spread made mainly from olives, traditionally black olives, capers and anchovies, pounded with olive oil. Green versions are also common. It is served on toast, with raw vegetables or as part of an aperitif spread. The name comes from tapeno, the Provençal word for capers.

Order it: As part of an apéritif with a glass of local wine. A little goes a long way because the flavour is intense.
12
A bold local dip

Anchoïade

Anchoïade is a punchy anchovy-based sauce or dip, usually made with anchovies, garlic and olive oil. It is served with raw vegetables, bread or sometimes warm vegetables. It captures the salty, savoury side of Mediterranean cooking and is a classic thing to share before a meal.

Order it: To share as an aperitif. If you enjoy anchovies, this is one of the region’s essential flavours.
13
A local pastry

Fougasse

Fougasse is a flat Provençal bread, often shaped with decorative slashes so it resembles a leaf. Depending on the bakery and town, it may be plain or flavoured with olives, herbs, anchovies, cheese or onions. It is a versatile snack for a picnic or something to put on the table with an aperitif.

Order it: From a bakery in the morning, when the crust is fresh. Ask which savoury fillings are made that day.

Seafood and dishes from the Mediterranean

Fishing harbours from Menton to Saint-Tropez shape the menus, although the catch and specialities change from one port to the next.

14
The famous Provençal fish stew

Bouillabaisse

Bouillabaisse is most closely associated with Marseille, west of the French Riviera, but it is found on menus across the wider Provençal coast. It is a substantial fish stew traditionally made with several types of local rockfish, aromatics, tomatoes, saffron and olive oil. It is often served in stages, with the broth, fish, croutons and rouille sauce. It is a dish with rules and history, not simply any seafood soup.

Order it: At a specialist restaurant and check whether it must be ordered in advance. Authentic versions are usually priced as a substantial meal for at least two.
15
Simple grilled fish

Poisson grillé du jour

Sometimes the best thing to order is the simplest: the fish of the day, grilled and served with lemon, olive oil or seasonal vegetables. Sea bream, sea bass, red mullet and other local catches may appear depending on the season and availability. A good harbour restaurant will often tell you what has come in that day.

Order it: Ask which fish is local and fresh, how it is priced (often by weight), and whether the price includes sides.
16
Seafood starter

Ourins, sardines and petits poissons

Depending on the season and the restaurant, you may see sea urchins, grilled sardines, anchovies, small fried fish or seafood platters. These are not available everywhere or all year, and local rules and conservation measures affect what can be served. Choose reputable restaurants and ask what is in season rather than expecting every item on every menu.

Order it: Follow the daily menu and ask where the catch comes from. Seasonal availability matters.

Menton and Monaco: two special tastes

At the eastern end of the Riviera, Italian influences are close at hand. Menton has its celebrated lemons, while Monaco has a small but distinctive culinary identity of its own.

17
Monaco's savoury speciality

Barbajuan

Barbajuan are little fried pastries traditionally associated with Monaco and the nearby Menton hinterland. The filling commonly includes Swiss chard, ricotta or other cheese, rice, herbs and sometimes pumpkin, depending on the family recipe. They are crisp outside, soft inside and often served as an appetiser or snack. The name is linked to the Monegasque dialect and is commonly explained as “Uncle John”.

Order it: As an aperitif or starter in Monaco, and look for them at local food celebrations.
18
The citrus of the Riviera

Citron de Menton

Menton's lemons are prized for their fragrant peel and distinctive local identity. You will find them in tarts, cakes, jams, marmalades, sorbets, liqueurs and savoury dishes. The Fête du Citron celebrates the fruit each year, but lemon treats are worth seeking out in Menton throughout the season and beyond.

Order it: Try a slice of lemon tart or a lemon-based dessert from a local pâtisserie. Look for products that specifically name Citron de Menton.

Save room for something sweet

The Riviera's bakeries and pâtisseries are a destination in their own right. These are the treats to look out for after lunch or with an afternoon coffee.

19
The Saint-Tropez icon

Tarte tropézienne

This famous dessert was created in Saint-Tropez in the 1950s by pâtissier Alexandre Micka. It is a round brioche split and filled with a rich cream, then finished with pearl sugar. Its name and popularity became closely linked with the town and the Riviera's glamorous summer years. It is indulgent, soft and best shared after a swim or a long lunch.

Order it: In Saint-Tropez, where the original story began, or from a recognised bakery elsewhere on the coast.
20
A Niçois bakery classic

Ganses and other local sweets

Ganses are delicate fried pastries from the Nice area, often twisted or knotted, dusted with sugar and flavoured with citrus blossom or other aromatics. They are especially associated with carnival time. Across the Riviera, also look for seasonal fruit tarts, almond biscuits, candied fruit and artisan ice cream. Calissons are more closely associated with Aix-en-Provence than the coast, but are widely sold across the region.

Order it: Ask a local baker what is traditional for the season. Some of the best sweets are only made at particular times of year.

Where to eat like a local on the French Riviera

Make the markets part of the meal

Nice: Cours Saleya and the old town are natural places to look for socca, pissaladière, olives and Niçois specialities.

Menton: Look for lemon products, local produce, fresh pasta and dishes that reflect the town's Italian borderland influences.

Antibes: Explore the covered market and old-town food shops for seasonal produce, tapenade, fougasse and prepared Provençal dishes.

Cannes: Around Forville market, look for vegetables, herbs, olives, seafood and local prepared foods.

Saint-Tropez: Try the town's famous tarte tropézienne and explore the market for Provençal produce and specialities.

Choose a short menu. A restaurant with a concise seasonal menu can be a good sign that the kitchen is cooking with what is available locally.

Look for the plat du jour. The daily special is often where you will find seasonal fish, vegetable dishes and slow-cooked Provençal cooking.

Eat at a traiteur or bakery. For a quick, delicious lunch, local prepared-food shops and bakeries are excellent places to find pissaladière, petits farcis, fougasse and savoury tarts.

Ask what is local and in season. Not every dish is available all year. Seafood, courgette flowers, summer vegetables and festive pastries all have their seasons.

Remember the lunch rhythm. Many traditional kitchens serve lunch for a limited period and may close between lunch and dinner. In popular towns and during summer, booking ahead is sensible.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most typical food in Nice?

Socca, salade niçoise, pan bagnat, pissaladière, petits farcis and tourte de blettes are among the best-known specialities of Nice and its surrounding area.

What should I eat in Menton?

Look for dishes and desserts featuring Citron de Menton, as well as fresh seafood, market produce and food influenced by the town's position close to the Italian border.

Is bouillabaisse a French Riviera dish?

Bouillabaisse is most famously associated with Marseille, in Provence to the west of the Côte d'Azur. You may find it elsewhere along the coast, but it is useful to know its Marseille roots.

What is a typical dessert from Saint-Tropez?

Tarte tropézienne is the best-known sweet speciality: a soft brioche filled with cream and topped with pearl sugar, created in Saint-Tropez in the 1950s.

Can I find vegetarian food on the French Riviera?

Yes. Socca, ratatouille, soupe au pistou, many versions of petits farcis and seasonal vegetable dishes can be vegetarian. Recipes vary, so check whether anchovies, meat or stock are included.
